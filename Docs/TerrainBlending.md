# Terrain blending

Spec for drawing the ground (grass, gravel, stone and the materials that come after them) with one shader that blends terrain layers, instead of one dual-grid tileset per layer. [Architecture.md](Architecture.md) describes what exists today; this file describes the target and the order in which to build it. Tick off the phase checklist as steps land, and move each settled behaviour into Architecture.md, [DualGrid.md](DualGrid.md) and [SceneGeneration.md](SceneGeneration.md) as its code arrives.

Every rule in [Multiplayer.md](Multiplayer.md#rules-for-new-code-starting-now) applies. Rendering is local: each machine draws the ground from terrain data it already has, so nothing here is replicated.

## Where we start

- Each area's `Grid` holds three ground layers, `GrassTilemap`, `GravelTilemap` and `StoneTilemap`. Each is a dual-grid data tilemap (`DualGridTilemapModule`) with a render tilemap of a 16-piece `DualGridRuleTile`, drawn on the `Terrain` sorting layer at orders 0, 1 and 2, so stone draws over gravel and gravel over grass. Grass is painted under the paths too.
- Navigation reads the data tilemaps: each has a `NavigationTerrainSource2D` with its `TerrainType2D` and priority (0, 1, 2), and the highest priority decides a cell. Stone and gravel cost the same as grass on purpose (see [SceneGeneration.md](SceneGeneration.md#terrain-layers-and-drawing-order)).
- Walls are the `CollisionTilemap`: a dual-grid walls module with grid colliders on the `Obstacles` layer, its render tilemap at order 10.
- Ground cells are written by `AreaLayoutWriter` (the scene generator), `AreaTerrain` (runtime edits, replicated by `NetworkAreaTerrain`), `StressTestMap`, and by hand with the Dual Grid Brush, which previews a stroke on the render tilemap while hovering. `Grass_Palette`, `Gravel_Palette` and `Stone_Palette` each hold their layer's data tile.
- A tile only knows whether its own layer is filled at its four corners, never what lies under its empty corners. Each path tileset's edge is therefore drawn for grass (an earthy rim, an outline, a green-tinted shadow), and where stone meets gravel the stone shows the same heavy border.
- The ground tilesets are 64 px per unit: `grass.png` (hand-drawn, its source not yet credited in [ArtCredits.md](ArtCredits.md)), and `gravel_path.png` and `stone_path.png`, generated by [tools/terrain-art/pathtiles.py](../tools/terrain-art/pathtiles.py). `walls.png` is a generated 64 px placeholder. Props and characters are 32 px per unit, and so is the Pixel Perfect Camera (assets PPU 32, reference resolution 640 x 360).
- Rendering is URP 17.4 with the 2D renderer. Areas have one Global Light 2D and no other lights. `Renderer2D.asset` has no renderer features, and the project has no custom shaders.

## Goals

- Every pair of ground materials meets with its own transition: stone over gravel is drawn differently from stone over grass, and grass over nothing (the map's edge) is one more pair.
- Two looks, both pixel-crisp, switched by a developer toggle: **crisp**, designed rounded edges with rims and outlines like today's tilesets, and **soft**, a height-driven blend.
- One pixel grid: every ground material and the walls at 32 px per unit, like props, characters and the camera.
- What the current tilesets guarantee still holds: nothing is drawn detached from its shape, edges agree across cell borders, and the drawn shapes follow the navigation cells.
- Runtime terrain edits change the picture on every machine, with nothing new replicated.
- Painting by hand keeps a preview of the stroke while hovering.
- Ground textures repeat less visibly than one-cell tiles do.
- A new material is a material asset and the recipe that generates its textures, not a tileset.

## Non-goals (for now)

- A custom render pipeline. See Decisions.
- Changing navigation, terrain costs, or the data tilemaps as the source of truth.
- Walls in the blend: they stay a dual-grid layer, because they are raised and carry colliders. Only their art moves to 32 px.
- A player-facing option for the look. The toggle is for developers.
- Normal maps, 2D shadows or other lights on the ground.
- Animated materials (water), elevation and cliffs.

## Decisions

- **Stay on URP's 2D renderer.** A custom pipeline would give full control of the frame, but Shader Graph only targets Built-in, URP and HDRP, and we would have to rebuild what we use today: 2D lights, the Pixel Perfect Camera, post-processing, screen-space UI and the Scene view's gizmos and previews, then maintain all of it across Unity upgrades. URP already gives what custom rendering needs: Shader Graph's Sprite Lit target with Custom Function nodes, hand-written HLSL on URP's 2D lighting includes, `ScriptableRendererFeature2D` passes injected at a chosen sorting layer, the camera sorting layer texture, and the Pixel Perfect Camera's upscale render texture. Revisit only if profiling shows the 2D renderer is the bottleneck, if a frame structure cannot be expressed with renderer features, or if URP upgrades keep breaking our shaders.
- **Shader logic lives in HLSL files**, called from thin Shader Graph wrappers, with shared helpers in `Assets/Shaders/Include` (pixel snapping, periodic noise, rounded corner distances). A later move to another pipeline would rewrite the wrappers and the lighting glue, not the logic.
- **The data tilemaps stay the source of truth.** The renderer only reads them. A layer's navigation priority is its place in the renderer's layers, which is also the draw order, so the layer drawn on top of a cell is the one that decides it for navigation: the renderer's inspector sets the priorities when the layers are reordered, a navigation source added to a layer starts with its layer's, the scene writer uses them, and the renderer logs an error while they disagree (and still draws, since the drawing never reads the priority).
- **One renderer per area**, `TerrainBlendRenderer` on the `Grid`, replacing the render tilemaps of every ground layer.
- **Control map:** a runtime texture with one texel per cell, holding one bit per ground layer (up to 8 layers in an `R8` texel), point-sampled. It is built from the data tilemaps when the renderer is enabled and patched cell by cell from `Tilemap.tilemapPositionsChanged`, with one `Apply` per frame at most. It is never saved, so scenes stay merge-friendly and no asset is written at runtime.
- **Mesh:** quads in chunks of 16 x 16 cells over the layers' bounds, on the `Terrain` sorting layer at order 0, so off-screen chunks are culled. Walls stay at order 10 and props on `Entities`.
- **Both looks share every input** (control map, textures, pair rules) and differ only in the blend function, picked by a shader keyword that a field on the renderer sets.
- **32 px per unit.** Ground textures are made at 32 px per cell. `walls.png` is regenerated at 32 px pieces (a 128 x 128 sheet, PPU 32). Grass is generated like the other materials, which also removes the uncredited art; if `grass.png`'s source turns up, it can be downscaled and cleaned up instead.
- **Grass is a layer like the others.** Where no layer is filled the renderer draws nothing, and grass over nothing is a pair rule.
- **Pair rules:** each material has default edge settings, and a pair (upper, lower) can override them.
- **Style asset:** materials and pair rules live in a `TerrainStyle` ScriptableObject. The renderer reads it, builds its runtime material and texture arrays from it, and never writes it. From Phase 1b each material is its own `TerrainMaterial` asset: the style keeps the shader, the shared settings and the pair overrides, keyed by material asset.
- **Painting:** ground layers become plain tilemaps holding one plain tile per layer (the palettes keep one tile each). A `TerrainBrush` previews the hovered cells by writing preview bits into the renderer's control map, never into the tilemap. The Dual Grid Brush stays for walls. (Phase 3 made the Terrain Brush a Dual Grid Brush that adds this preview, and the Tile Palette's default; the Dual Grid Brush stays in the list. See the Phase 3 notes.)
- **Materials are generated by algorithms**, which saves manual work, and their textures are kept as persistent files. Phases 0 and 1 used standard-library Python in [tools/terrain-art](../tools/terrain-art); from Phase 1b an editor tool generates each material's textures from an editor-only recipe asset (seed and parameters), with a preview in its inspector. The shader depends on the cell fields, so generation must be reproducible.

## Behaviour

### Shared per pixel

1. Snap the pixel to the 32 px-per-unit grid, so every edge lands on whole pixels.
2. Find the corner nearest to the pixel and read the four cells around it from the control map (the dual-grid idea, done in the shader).
3. For each layer, from the bottom up, decide whether it covers the pixel (below), and composite. When a layer is drawn over another, the shader knows both materials, so it applies that pair's rule.
4. Interiors sample each material's albedo texture, which repeats every N cells (N per material, 4 by default). Shifting a material's texture by a random offset per cell, to break the repeat further, was tried in Phase 2 and left out (see the Phase 2 notes).
5. Lighting comes from the Sprite Lit target, as for every sprite.

### Crisp

The current tilesets' maths, moved into the shader (see [DualGrid.md](DualGrid.md#paths) and `pathtiles.py`):

- A layer's shape at the pixel is the exact signed distance to its filled cells around the corner: an outer corner rounded, an inner one filleted. Both radii stay under half a cell (16 px at 32 px per cell; about 14 and 11 px, scaled from today's 29 and 22 at 64 px), so a boundary is straight where it crosses a cell border and neighbouring cells agree.
- Smooth noise made of long waves that repeat with the texture moves the edge by about a pixel. Its slope stays below 1, so the distance keeps rising away from the shape everywhere and no blob can pinch off.
- From the outside in, the pair rule draws a translucent shadow, an outline and a rim before the interior.
- Each material may have a cell field texture beside its albedo: for every pixel, the offset to the centre of the chip or stone it belongs to. A chip is drawn whole or not at all, decided by the distance at its centre; a cobble whose centre lies near the rim is left out, and the others are cut along the rim's smooth line. Decisions taken at a cell centre agree across cell borders.

### Soft

- Each layer's weight at the pixel is interpolated from the four corner cells (1 where filled, 0 where not), plus the material's height texture times a height scale, the heights mixed by the weight of each cell's top layer. Layers are composited bottom up, and a layer covers the pixel where its weight passes a threshold, with an optional band of one or two pixels for a rim.
- The height term may move a boundary by a few pixels at most, and its slope must stay below the weight's own slope across a cell (1/32 per pixel), for the same reason as in crisp: no detached blobs.
- No translucent alpha is used inside the ground: the result stays pixel-crisp, only its shape differs from crisp's. The edge pixel is opaque and no shadow is drawn outside the edge; tufts grow out of straight edges as in crisp.

### Pair rules to start with

| Upper over lower | Crisp | Soft |
|---|---|---|
| Grass over nothing | Outline and a lighter fringe, like today's grass edge | Same colours, height-blended shape |
| Gravel over grass | Darker dirt rim, outline, soft shadow (today's look) | Dirt rim band |
| Stone over grass | Earthy rim, outline, soft shadow (today's look) | Mortar rim band |
| Stone over gravel | Thin mortar, neutral shadow, no outline: cobbles set into the gravel | Cobbles' height decides which stones show |

### Edits and preview

- A runtime edit through `AreaTerrain`, on the host or applied on a client from `NetworkAreaTerrain`, changes a data tilemap; the renderer patches those texels and applies once at the end of the frame.
- In the editor the renderer runs in edit mode too, so the generator's Generate, painting and undo show at once.
- `TerrainBrush` writes preview bits for the hovered stroke into the control map, through the renderer, and clears them when the stroke ends or the pointer leaves; the tilemap changes only on click.

### Developer toggle

`TerrainBlendRenderer` has a `mode` field (crisp or soft) that sets the shader keyword on its runtime material, in edit mode and in play mode. It is not saved per machine and not exposed to players.

## Architecture

### Runtime (Gameplay, `Assets/Scripts/World/Terrain`)

| Type | Role |
|---|---|
| `TerrainControlMap` | Plain C#. Knows the layers' bounds, packs each cell's layer bits, grows when an edit lands outside the bounds, collects changed cells and writes them into its `Texture2D` in one batch, and holds preview bits separately from the real ones. Testable in EditMode. |
| `TerrainBlendRenderer` | `MonoBehaviour` on the area's `Grid`, `[ExecuteAlways]`. Lists the ground layers bottom first (tilemap and `TerrainMaterial` asset), its `TerrainStyle`, chunk size and `mode`. Builds the control map, the chunk meshes and a runtime material from the style; subscribes to `Tilemap.tilemapPositionsChanged` in `OnEnable` and unsubscribes in `OnDisable`; destroys its texture, meshes and material when disabled. No per-frame work unless cells changed. Shows a brush's preview bits (`SetPreview`, `ClearPreview`). |
| `TerrainMaterial` | ScriptableObject, one per material: albedo, optional cell field and height textures, repeat in cells, corner radii, chip settings, base colour, the soft look's height scale and default edge settings. Read-only at runtime; its recipe writes the textures, repeat, cell mode and base colour in the editor. |
| `TerrainStyle` | ScriptableObject: the shader, the shared settings and the pair overrides, keyed by material asset. Read-only at runtime. |

Shaders in `Assets/Shaders`: `Terrain/TerrainBlend.shadergraph` (Sprite Lit, a Custom Function node calling `Terrain/TerrainBlend.hlsl`) and shared helpers in `Include/`.

### Editor (`TopDownRPG.Editor`)

- `TerrainBrush`: a grid brush for ground layers with the hover preview described above (`TerrainBrushEditor`); elsewhere the Dual Grid Brush.
- `TerrainTileWriter`: the plain tile of each material, with a swatch of its albedo for the palette (Phase 3).
- `TerrainMaterialRecipe` and its kinds (grass tufts, gravel chips, stone cobbles): one editor-only asset per material with its seed and parameters, whose inspector previews the textures; `TerrainMaterialWriter` writes them as the material's textures (Phase 1b).
- `AreaLayoutWriter` and `AreaGenerator.TerrainLayer`: layers become plain data tilemaps with a plain tile, a terrain type, a navigation priority and a material asset, instead of a rule tile and a sorting order; the writer adds or updates the area's `TerrainBlendRenderer`.
- A migration command that converts an area's ground layers: removes the modules and render tilemaps, swaps the data tiles for plain ones, and adds the renderer.

### Art

The material generator writes, per material, an albedo texture and, where the material has chips or stones, a cell field texture of the same size; a height texture for soft mode; and the 32 px walls sheet. Generated textures are imported with point filtering, no compression and no mipmaps, at 32 PPU. The generator is an editor tool in `TopDownRPG.Editor`, driven by one recipe asset per material; until Phase 1b it was `tools/terrain-art/materials.py`. `compose.py` still previews the old tilesets on test maps.

### Networking

Nothing new. The renderer derives everything from the data tilemaps, which `AreaTerrain` already keeps in step on every machine, and a client's late join already receives every edit.

## Removals and migrations

- The ground layers' `DualGridTilemapModule`s and render tilemaps in `Area_Clearing`, `Area_VillageForest` and `Area_StressTest` (`StressTestMap` paints ground through a module today).
- `grass_DualGridRuleTile`, `gravel_path_DualGridRuleTile`, `stone_path_DualGridRuleTile` and their tilesets, replaced by material textures; the palettes hold plain tiles (done in Phase 3).
- `walls.png` regenerated at 32 px; the walls rule tile keeps its 16 slices.
- `tools/terrain-art/materials.py`, once the editor generator replaces it (done in Phase 1b); `pathtiles.py` and `compose.py` once the old path tilesets go (Phase 4).
- `AreaGenerator.TerrainLayer` fields change (keep serialized names or add `[FormerlySerializedAs]`), and the generator assets follow.
- Tests that inspect the ground's render tilemaps (`AreaGenerationTests`' writer test) check the renderer instead. Wall rendering tests stay.

#### Phase 0 decision note

The spike (removed after the Phase 1 review; see the `e9f87f3` and `f6a62fb` commits) was run on `Area_VillageForest` and `Area_Clearing`: menu `Tools/TopDownRPG/Dev/Terrain Spike/` (Attach To Active Area, Toggle Visible, Flip Look, Detach). Its textures came from `tools/terrain-art/materials.py`, which the editor's material recipes replaced in Phase 1b. Measurements were taken with `TerrainSpikeProbe` on an RTX 5070 Ti under D3D12.

Answers:

1. **Lighting and sorting: yes.** A `MeshRenderer` quad per 16 x 16 cells with the Sprite Lit graph is lit by the Global Light 2D and by a point Light 2D exactly as the render tilemaps are, and sorts by its sorting layer and order: a `Terrain` sprite at order 3 is hidden under the spike at order 5, one at order 10 (the walls' order) and everything on `Entities` draw over it. Like any sprite, it is lit only on sorting layers a light targets (on `Overlay`, which the Global Light 2D leaves out, it renders black). Neither SpriteRenderers nor a TilemapRenderer is needed. The graph turns on Disable Color Tint, since a mesh has no sprite colour.
2. **Texture arrays.** One `Texture2DArray` per kind (albedo, cell field, height), a slice per material, built at runtime with `Graphics.CopyTexture` from 128 px RGBA32 textures imported with sRGB off; the HLSL treats albedo bytes as sRGB itself, so every copy is between identical formats. The shader reads them with `Load` and needs no sampler. In the spike each material still has its own branch, because grass, gravel and stone each have their own interior rules, and each branch reads a fixed slice. The arrays keep the material's property list the same as materials are added, where fixed slots would add three texture properties per material, and they let Phase 1 pick the slice from the style once materials share one set of interior rules. Every slice must share size and format, so materials stay 128 px; the renderer refuses any other size.
3. **Shimmer: none, except at exact half-pixel camera offsets.** At 1920 x 1080 and zoom 3 the camera was moved by fractions of a screen pixel and each frame compared with the unmoved frame shifted by whole pixels. The spike matched exactly at 0.25, 0.75, 1 and 2.3 px, so it only ever jumps by whole pixels, like a 32 px sprite. At exactly 0.5 and 1.5 px about 16 % of the pixels differed: pixel centres fall exactly on art-pixel edges, so `floor` picks either side and some art pixels come out 2 or 4 screen pixels wide instead of 3. Today's render tilemaps differed at every offset (16 to 18 % of the pixels at 0.25, 0.5, 1.5 and 2.3 px, 3 % at 0.75 px, 0.2 % even at 1 px), because a 64 px texel is 1.5 screen pixels at zoom 3: today's ground already shimmers, and 32 px fixes that. The camera does not snap now (`CameraFollow2D.snapToPixelGrid` is off in `Main`, and the Pixel Perfect Camera's grid and pixel snapping are off), so it passes through half-pixel offsets, and an odd viewport size could keep it on one.
4. **Cost: negligible.** Drawing the whole Village Forest at 7680 x 4320 (all 30 chunks) took 1.41 ms per frame without the spike and 1.80 ms with it, in either look: about 0.4 ms per 33 million pixels, or 0.025 ms for a 1080p frame on this GPU (at 1080p itself the difference was below the editor's noise). A 640 x 360 view touches at most 6 chunks, so 6 draw calls. Nothing runs per frame on the CPU; a full rebuild (control map, arrays, chunks) takes 3.5 ms in the editor, and the textures take 6 KB (control map) plus 576 KB (three arrays of three 128 px slices). A pixel reads 4 control texels, up to 3 heights in soft, the albedo and field of each visible layer, and 4 more control texels per chip or stone decided at its centre.

Both looks render both areas with every edge attached: `TerrainSpikeProbe.CheckBlobs` labels the topmost visible layer of every pixel and finds, in both looks, one region per path and per grass part (Village Forest: grass 2, gravel 2, stone 1; Clearing: grass 4, gravel 2, stone 1), none under 200 px, and no holes. Crisp follows today's shapes cell for cell, with a thin mortar line and a neutral shadow where stone meets gravel instead of today's heavy border. Soft turns cell steps into straight diagonals and chamfered corners, waves its edges by the materials' heights, and lets whole cobbles make the stone-over-gravel edge.

Phase 1 should keep:
- Chunk quads with a Sprite Lit graph whose Custom Function (file mode) calls the HLSL, on the `Terrain` sorting layer.
- Cumulative layers: a layer counts a cell as filled when it or any layer above it is filled, so lower layers continue under upper ones. Stone beside gravel then shows no seam, and gravel fills the notch beside a stone corner.
- Pair choice per pixel from the layers' distances: a lower layer is "under" an upper one only where its edge lies at least 1.5 px beyond the upper's. Where the edges coincide (gravel continuing under a stone path that borders grass), the lower layer is skipped there, so the edge reads as stone over grass. This needs the same edge noise on every layer in crisp; soft mixes the materials' heights by the bilinear weight of each cell's top layer, which keeps coinciding edges equal too.
- Chip and stone decisions from the distance at their centre, measured with the corner patch around that centre.
- Soft weights interpolated bilinearly, minus a bump on diagonal pairs so two diagonal cells never join through the saddle point.

Phase 1 should change:
- The pair switches abruptly where that 1.5 px margin is crossed, which shows at three-way corners (a light stone wedge at about (2.9, -4.2) in the Village Forest). Blend the pair settings over a pixel or two.
- Cobbles dropped near the rim leave mortar bumps 3 to 4 px wide at 32 px. The spike already drops fewer than `pathtiles.py` (centre within rim + 1 px instead of rim + 1.75); cut more of them instead.
- Grass over nothing has the outline and light bands but not today's spiky tufts, which an edge term with a slope under 1 cannot make. If they are wanted, draw them as an attached decoration in the outer band.
- The textures need an art pass: the gravel chips are blocky at 32 px and the grass tufts faint.
- Snap the camera to the 1/32 grid (`CameraFollow2D` has the option; it is a change to `Main`), so the half-pixel case cannot happen.
- The spike fills cells with no ground with a backdrop colour so today's tiles cannot show through; the real renderer draws nothing there. Tile edits through `Tilemap.tilemapPositionsChanged` are wired in the spike but were not exercised.
- Editor checks have to allow for three things. Render requests skip shader variants still compiling in the background, so compile them first with `ShaderUtil.CompilePass`. Renderers created, reactivated or moved to another layer earlier in the same editor update are not drawn until the next one. A Light 2D builds its mesh in `LateUpdate`, which an unfocused editor seldom runs.

#### Phase 1 notes

The code is described in [Architecture.md](Architecture.md#world-and-areas). `Area_VillageForest` is converted: a `TerrainBlendRenderer` on its `Grid` draws grass, gravel and stone with `Assets/Settings/Terrain/TerrainStyle.asset`, and the three ground `TilemapRenderer`s are switched off; the modules, rule tiles and render tilemaps stay until Phase 4. What settled differently from the plan above or from the Phase 0 note:

- **Edge settings are a lookup texture** (`TerrainBlendLut`): a row of shared settings, a row per layer's material and a row per pair. A pair's rim is a gradient sampled every half pixel rather than one colour, so grass over nothing keeps today's light fringe bands (highlight, lime, a dark line, mid green) as data, and gravel's rim fades into the dirt.
- **Corner radii belong to the material, not to the pair.** A layer's shape is computed before the shader knows which pair is drawn there, and layers whose edges coincide must share their radii, or the lower one shows at corners (radii 4 px apart leave a sliver of about 1.7 px, more than the pair margin).
- **Pair settings blend** over the style's pair blend (1.5 px) around the 1.5 px margin, while whether a lower layer is drawn still switches at the margin. The light stone wedge at the three-way corner near (2.9, -4.2) is gone.
- **Chips and stones decide at their centre** both whether they are kept and which pair, and so which rim, they meet, so a stone stays whole where the pair changes. Stones are left out only where their centre lies more than 0.5 px into the rim (the spike needed it 1 px clear of the rim); the others are cut along the rim behind a 0.5 px mortar line and bevelled to the light. A gravel chip is kept only where all of it lies clear of the rim, so kept chips are never painted over: the cell field holds each chip's reach from its centre pixel, which the shader stretches by the edge noise's steepest slope, since the distance can fall that much faster than the pixels move away. Small chips can therefore lie closer to the edge than big ones, and the path's edge keeps a band of bare dirt a few pixels wide.
- **Tufts are back on grass over nothing**, as a decoration of the edge and the band outside it: five triangular spikes per corner patch, placed along the edge by the pixel's position along the bare shape's tangent, so each spike stands straight out of the edge and every pixel between its tip and the edge belongs to it. Tuft pixels are opaque, outlined, and filled with the rim's outer colour. They keep to straight edges and fade out within a few degrees of curve: around a corner the normals spread or close up, and the blob check found spike tips there touching the edge by a pixel corner only. They are smaller than today's (up to 4.5 px).
- **The control map uploads before the next render**, not at the end of the frame: a tile change patches the map at once and subscribes the renderer to `RenderPipelineManager.beginContextRendering`, which uploads and unsubscribes. An undo restores tilemaps without reporting the cells it changed, so in the editor the renderer reads its layers again on `Undo.undoRedoPerformed`.
- **Chunks sit on a fixed 16-cell grid**, so growing bounds only add chunks, and the control map grows by 8 cells more than needed on each side that grows, so painting outward does not regrow it every cell.
- **Texture arrays have a slice per layer**, sized to the largest material, so materials may repeat over different numbers of cells (each texture is its repeat x 32 px). No height array is built until Phase 2.
- **The camera does not snap after all.** Turning on `CameraFollow2D.snapToPixelGrid` in `Main`, as the Phase 0 note suggested, made the moving player shake, and it was turned back off. The camera follows the player smoothly and the player's body is interpolated, so a snapped camera jumps by whole art pixels (3 screen pixels at zoom 3) while the player moves by fractions of one: simulated at 5 units per second, the player wobbles against the screen by 1.4 screen pixels on average and up to 2 at 60 fps (2.7 at 144 fps), against 0.3 without snapping. Avoiding the half-pixel case needs the sprites snapped as well (the Pixel Perfect Camera's pixel snapping or upscale render texture) or a camera that snaps only once it has settled; that is still to decide.
- **Art**: gravel chips are rounded (tilted ellipses about 2 to 5 px across, lit from the top left, each with a one-pixel shadow that belongs to its chip, so a chip left out takes its shadow with it), and grass tufts are darker with lighter tips. `compose.py` was not extended: `TerrainBlendProbe.CheckBlobs` checks the real renderer on real areas instead.

Measured with `TerrainBlendProbe` on the same RTX 5070 Ti under D3D12:

- Blob check on the Village Forest, tufts included: one region per path and grass part (grass 2, gravel 2, stone 1, as in Phase 0), no blob under 200 px, no holes.
- Lighting: a grass texel of (35, 114, 40) renders as (32, 105, 36) under the Global Light 2D, as on today's tiles.
- Shimmer, 1920 x 1080 at zoom 3: a moved frame matches the reference shifted by whole pixels exactly, unless the camera's sub-pixel position crosses the half-pixel phase of the art grid on the way, where about 27 % of the pixels differ. Phase 0 started from a camera aligned with the art grid, so its mismatches fell at exactly 0.5 and 1.5 px. With the camera not snapping (see above), the blended ground changes shape at those moments like every 32 px sprite does; it no longer shimmers at every offset as the 64 px tiles did.
- Cost: drawing the whole area at 7680 x 4320 adds about 0.5 ms per frame (best of six runs: 1.13 ms without the chunks, 1.65 ms with them; Phase 0 measured 0.4 ms), and at 1920 x 1080 and zoom 3 about 0.2 ms, which is within the editor's noise. A full rebuild takes 4.7 ms.
- Editor checks have two more things to allow for. Hiding props through `forceRenderingOff` marks them changed, and the next save then writes every prop's `YPositionSorter` order into the scene, so the probe clears that mark. Chunks rebuilt after an inspector change appear one editor update later.

### Phase 1b: materials and generator in the editor

Generated textures stay, since they save manual work, but they move into the editor: each material and its textures become persistent assets, and the generator becomes an editor tool instead of a Python script. This comes before the soft look, whose height textures and per-cobble random values are generated and want tuning with a preview, and before the migration, whose generator assets and scene writer can then refer to material assets. Pair overrides keyed by material index, fragile when materials are reordered, move to material assets while only the Village Forest uses the style.

#### Phase 1b notes

The code is described in [Architecture.md](Architecture.md#world-and-areas) (World and areas, Editor tools). The three materials are assets in `Assets/Settings/Terrain/Materials`, created from the Phase 1 style's values; `TerrainStyle.asset` keeps the shader and the shared settings, and the Village Forest's renderer layers name the material assets (the scene's only change). The recipes in `Assets/Settings/Terrain/Recipes` keep `materials.py`'s parameters and seeds (20261012 grass, 20261013 gravel, 20261014 stone), and their Generate rewrote the three materials' textures. What settled differently from the plan above, or is worth knowing:

- **A material's name is its asset name**, and new texture files are named after it in lower case (`grass_albedo.png`). Once a material uses PNGs in `Assets/Textures/Terrain`, Generate keeps writing those, so renaming the material keeps its files and GUIDs. Generate refuses to write a file that is another material's texture, and writes all of a material's files or none.
- **Pair overrides are keyed by asset**, so one whose lower material goes missing (its asset deleted) would read as an override over nothing under Unity's `==`. The style tells a missing lower material from none, never applies such an override and warns about it.
- **Generate writes the repeat and the cell mode too**, not only the base colour: the textures' size follows the recipe's repeat and the meaning of the cell field's alpha follows the recipe's kind, so a material cannot disagree with its textures.
- **The recipe generates, a writer writes**, as `AreaGenerator.Generate` and `AreaLayoutWriter` split the scene generator: the recipe returns pixel arrays without touching an asset, and `TerrainMaterialWriter` writes the PNGs, reimports them and sets the material through its serialized data. It does so without undo, since the files cannot be undone either, then has the renderers drawing the material rebuild even when nothing on the material changed, since the textures' pixels may have.
- **Pixels are generated y up**, as `Texture2D` stores them, so offsets need no flipping, the light comes from (-0.6, 0.8) as in the shader, and a tuft glyph's top row is drawn highest.
- **A chip's or stone's centre pixel is its pixel nearest its seed**, not the pixel holding the seed: in `materials.py` an overlapping chip could take that pixel, leaving a chip pointing at another chip's pixel. A stone's distance to its cell edge is exact rather than taken from the nine nearest seeds: the seed grid is searched ring by ring around each pixel until no farther ring can hold a nearer seed or edge, which keeps a pixel's cost independent of the number of stones. Shading rounds halves up and adds the base shade as a whole number, so the field's blue channel and the albedo's bare dirt or mortar agree exactly.
- **Height waves too steep are scaled down** so the red channel's slope stays under 0.1 per pixel, where `materials.py` stopped on an assertion at the same limit. The contract allows 0.2; `materials.py`'s docstring said 0.2 while its check held half that.
- **Waves count whole periods across the texture**, as in `materials.py`, so they stay periodic at any repeat; changing a recipe's repeat changes their scale. Spots and stones per cell are rounded to a whole number across the texture.
- **The walls are not a recipe yet**: Phase 4's 32 px walls sheet is still to be made with this tool.

#### Phase 2 notes

The code is described in [Architecture.md](Architecture.md#world-and-areas). Both looks draw from the same control map, arrays and lookup texture; `TerrainBlendRenderer.mode` (Crisp or Soft) only switches the `_TERRAIN_SOFT` keyword on the runtime material. The Village Forest stays Crisp in its scene. The materials got their soft settings (height scale 2.5 px for grass, 2 for gravel, 1.5 for stone; a 1.5 px band of dirt (126, 104, 76) for gravel and of mortar (98, 88, 76) for stone) and `TerrainStyle.asset` its first pair override, stone over gravel. What settled differently from the plan above, the decisions taken, and what is worth knowing:

- **The keyword is a local, fragment-only multi_compile** (`#pragma multi_compile_local_fragment _ _TERRAIN_SOFT`, the graph keyword "Terrain Soft", not exposed on the material). The runtime material is made in code, so no material asset ever enables the keyword: as a `shader_feature`, builds would strip the soft variant and the toggle would do nothing in a development build. `multi_compile` keeps both variants wherever the toggle can flip. Local, because the keyword belongs to one material and should not take a global keyword slot; fragment-only, because only the fragment stage reads it, so the vertex variants are not doubled. In the editor the soft variant compiles in the background the first time it is drawn, so the ground disappears for a few frames after the first flip of a session.
- **The mode is serialized**, so the inspector shows it and it survives entering play mode and script reloads. Setting `Mode` from code switches the keyword without marking the scene changed; the inspector marks it changed like any field. `OnValidate` switches the keyword and rebuilds only when something the build reads changed since the last build (the style, the layers' tilemaps and materials, the chunk size, the sorting), so a flip from the inspector rebuilds nothing either. Switching takes about 4 microseconds and allocates nothing. The keyword is looked up again on every switch: one kept from the build stopped switching anything once the shader was reimported.
- **The soft distance** is `0.5 - (32 (w - 0.5) + h)` in pixels, positive outside: `w` the bilinear weight of the four cells (cumulative masks, as in crisp) less `4 s (1 - s) t (1 - t)` on diagonal pairs, which leaves 0.25 at the saddle, and `h` the heights of the filled cells' top layers mixed by their bilinear weights. A layer covers a pixel where the distance falls below 0.5, which is where crisp's edge pixel ends, so the pair margin, the pair blend, the bands from the edge inwards and the chip and stone decisions are crisp's code. Dividing by the weight's slope, to make the distance closer to Euclidean, was worked through and dropped: near inner corners the slope falls to 0.78 and the quotient becomes steeper than the raw distance.
- **The height term** is `scale (2 R - 1)`, so a red channel of 0.5 moves nothing. The recipes' red channels span about 0.09 to 0.87, so edges move by up to about 2 px for grass, 1.5 for gravel and 1.25 for stone. A material without a height texture gets a height scale of 0 in the lookup texture and its slice of the height array stays empty: no error, since crisp does not need the texture and a flip cannot rebuild.
- **The bound on the height scale.** A blob stays attached when every covered pixel is reached from the inside along the weight's slope, which holds when the height's slope stays below the weight's wherever the boundary can lie, that is where `|32 (w - 0.5)| <= scale`. The weight's slope there, in pixels per pixel, is 1 along a straight edge, at least 1.27 for a diagonal pair with its bump, and at least `sqrt(1 - scale / 16)` for one cell or three (on their diagonal, at the far end of the band). The height's slope is at most `2 scale ρ + scale Σ|∇β| / 32`, with ρ the red channel's slope and `β` the cells' mixing weights, whose gradients sum to at most about 3 per cell in the band (and only matter where materials of different heights meet). A numeric sweep over the four corner shapes gives `scale < 3.08 px` for ρ = 0.1 (the recipes' limit) and `scale < 1.9 px` for ρ = 0.2 (what the height contract has allowed so far). With the bytes' rounding (neighbours differ by up to 0.1 + 1/255) the margin at 3 px is gone; at 2.5 px the height's slope reaches 0.76 against the weight's 0.92 at the worst point, an inner corner. `TerrainMaterial.MaxHeightScale` is 2.5: the inspector's range, the getter and the lookup texture hold to it, and `OnValidate` warns about an edited asset holding more. The recipes' textures stay far below their limit (the steepest step between neighbours is 6/255).
- **The edge pixel is opaque** (the outline, the band's colour, or the rim's outer colour), and the outline does not fade into the interior as in crisp.
- **No shadow in soft.** A one-pixel band at the pair's shadow alpha was tried. It is the only translucent part left (at the map's edge it leaves translucent pixels over nothing), and along the steps of soft diagonals it reads as a second outline rather than as depth.
- **Tufts stay in soft.** Both looks grow them only along straight edges; soft edges are straight wherever the cells line up and diagonal elsewhere, which is where crisp's rounded steps leave them out too, so they show in about the same places. Their tangent comes from the bare weight's slope; the edge waves with the height, but the slope bound keeps the distance rising along that direction, so every spike stays attached (the blob checks below include them).
- **A band replaces the outline and the rim** in soft where the pair sets one (`EdgeSettings.softBandWidth` and `softBandColour`), shaded per pixel by the cell field's grit like the base colour; its width is the rim width that chips and stones are decided against. Grass over nothing has no band: it keeps the outline and the fringe gradient, the same colours as crisp.
- **Hard band, not a dither** (the open question). A one-pixel ordered dither (a checker over the band's inner pixel) was tried against the hard band: on straight runs it reads as a stitched seam, more visible than the band itself at zoom 3, and on diagonals it breaks the steps into dots, while the chips already give the dirt its noise. The band ends hard.
- **Whole chips in soft are measured where they come nearest the edge**: a chip is kept when the depth at its centre moved by its reach along the edge's outward direction there lies the keep margin clear of the band. Crisp's global stretch would have been too wide here: the soft distance's slope reaches 2.15 per pixel near diagonal pairs (from the bump) and corners, which would strip the chips from one-cell paths, while over a chip of reach 5 px the slope never exceeds its slope at the centre by more than 0.08. The depth at that point includes the height there.
- **Whole stones** (`EdgeSettings.softWholeStones`, over a material with cut stones): a stone is drawn whole, inside or outside the shape, when its centre's depth plus `scale (2 G - 1)` (the height's green channel, one value per stone) reaches the keep margin, with no rim, and is clear otherwise so the gravel shows between the stones. A layer that can show whole stones is never taken as the opaque layer the compositing starts from. With stone's scale of 1.5 px a stone moves by at most 1.5 px either way, under half the stone spacing (about 7 px), so a kept stone has a kept neighbour deeper inside and a left-out one a left-out neighbour further out. The first blob check found an 8 px gravel sliver at (3.04, -4.64), at the three-way corner, where whole stones reached across a gravel strip thinning out between stone and grass; whole stones are now used only where the stone's centre lies at least 10 px inside the lower layer, and nearer, the pair's rim cuts the stones as in crisp.
- **Stone over gravel** is the style's override: in crisp a 0.75 px rim of mortar (98, 88, 76), no outline and a neutral shadow (46, 40, 32) at 0.28 over a pixel, stones cut behind the stone material's 0.5 px gap, so the cobbles look set into the gravel; in soft whole stones.
- **Per-cell texture offsets were left out.** Tried on grass, the only candidate (a per-cell shift cuts any chip or stone across a cell border): a whole-pixel offset per world cell from a hash. The grass albedo's tint waves are continuous across the texture, so the shift breaks them at every cell border into a grid of 32 px squares, much more visible than the 4-cell repeat it hides (faint tint bands every 128 px), and it cut the tufts lying across borders. Breaking the repeat further needs another approach (longer repeats, or blending two offsets), not a per-cell shift.
- **The lookup texture** gained a column per pair (the band) and its ramp moved one column right; layer rows hold the height scale and whether the layer can show whole stones (see `TerrainBlendLut`).

### Phase 3: brush

- [x] `TerrainBrush` with the hover preview; palettes with plain tiles.

#### Phase 3 notes

The code is described in [Architecture.md](Architecture.md#world-and-areas) (World and areas, Editor tools). `TerrainBrush` is the Tile Palette's default brush, and `Grass_Palette`, `Gravel_Palette` and `Stone_Palette` hold the plain tiles `Tile_Grass`, `Tile_Gravel` and `Tile_Stone` from `Assets/Tiles/Ground`. No scene changed. What settled differently from the plan above, the decisions taken, and what is worth knowing:

- **The Terrain Brush is a Dual Grid Brush with a preview.** `TerrainBrush` derives from `DualGridBrush` without overriding anything, and `TerrainBrushEditor` derives from `DualGridBrushEditor`. The brush writes what the Dual Grid Brush writes. The editor adds the preview on ground layers: tilemaps for which `TerrainBlendRenderer.FindLayerDrawing` finds a renderer that is drawing them right now (`IsDrawing`: enabled, built, and built with that tilemap among its layers). On the layers of a renderer that is off or could not build, the editor falls back to the Dual Grid Brush's preview. On a layer that still has a module, the brush writes the module's data tile into every cell of the stroke, even where the brush holds no tile (the Dual Grid Brush always did), so Phase 4 finds one tile type there. On a plain layer it writes the palette's tile into the cells whose brush cell holds one, as Unity's brush does.
- **On a tilemap no renderer draws it is the Dual Grid Brush, not a plain grid brush.** On a plain tilemap the two are the same, since the Dual Grid Brush falls back to the grid brush there. A dual-grid tilemap (the walls, the Clearing's and the stress test's ground) is where they differ. A plain grid brush would show no preview there, since a data tilemap has no renderer, and it would write whatever the palette holds, which after the palette switch is a plain tile. As the Dual Grid Brush it writes the module's data tile and previews on the render tilemap. One brush therefore paints every tilemap of an area.
- **Default brush** (approved): `TerrainBrush` is `[CustomGridBrush(true, true, true, "Terrain Brush")]`, and `DualGridBrush` became `(true, false, false)`. The Dual Grid Brush stays in the brush list for walls, and Unity's default brush stays hidden. Unity keeps the picked brush only for the editor session, so each session starts on the default. Only one brush may be the default, and it must hide its own default instance or the list shows it twice.
- **Palettes switched** (approved): the three ground palettes hold the plain tiles, written into the palette prefabs with `PrefabUtility`. Only the tile and sprite references changed. `Walls_Palette` keeps the walls' data tile. Nothing painted in the unmigrated areas changes: both brushes write the module's data tile on a dual-grid layer, and `AreaTerrain`, the generator and the stress map write data tiles. Only the brushes that write the picked tile as it is (Line, Random, Group) could put a plain tile into a dual-grid layer. The module treats any tile as filled, and the Phase 4 migration swaps the tiles anyway.
- **Swatches are generated.** `TerrainTileWriter` writes each material's tile, without a collider, and its swatch: the bottom-left 32 px cell of the albedo, saved as `<material>_swatch.png` in `Assets/Sprites/Terrain`. `PixelArtTextureDefaults` imports it as a 32 PPU, point-filtered sRGB sprite, and the writer makes it a single sprite. Run it from `Tools/TopDownRPG/World/Terrain/Write Ground Tiles`. The material writer's Generate rewrites the swatch of a material that has a tile and points the tile at it again, and it leaves a swatch that already holds the same bytes untouched; a second Generate left every file unchanged. Only the materials in `Assets/Settings/Terrain/Materials` get tiles, since tiles and swatches are named after materials and names are unique only within that folder. Ground data tilemaps never draw the swatches. No data tilemap has a `TilemapRenderer` in the Village Forest, the Clearing, the stress test or `VillageTest`, the scene writer adds none, and the renderer now warns about a layer whose tilemap has an enabled one.
- **Flood fill keeps the default behaviour, with no preview on a ground layer.** It is the Dual Grid Brush's flood fill: `Tilemap.FloodFill` with the module's data tile, or the grid brush's with the palette's tile. It has undo, and the renderer and navigation follow it as they follow any edit. A ground layer is only filled or empty, so a flood from an empty cell fills the whole empty region. Outside a shape that region reaches the tilemap's bounds, so on terrain a flood makes a rectangle of ground, and holes are as quick to box fill with a preview. A faithful preview would have to reproduce `Tilemap.FloodFill`'s native rule (same tile, within the tilemap's bounds), or the brush would have to fill by itself. Either way it would recompute a region as large as the layer on every hovered cell. The grid brush's own flood preview puts editor preview tiles on the data tilemap, which is invisible there, so it is skipped too.
- **Preview API.** `TerrainBlendRenderer.SetPreview(tilemap, cells, filled)`, `ClearPreview()` and `IsDrawing(tilemap)` are internal; the editor assembly sees them. `SetPreview` replaces any earlier preview, patches the control map's preview bits, adds chunks when filled preview cells grow the bounds, and schedules the upload before the next render like a tile change. It finds the layer among the tilemaps the renderer was built from, as tile changes do, not in the inspector's list. Between an inspector change to the layers and the rebuild it schedules, a reordered tilemap therefore still previews its built layer, and a tilemap just added gets no preview (`SetPreview` returns false and changes nothing). The bounds and chunks a preview grew stay while previews replace each other, so hovering outward does not shrink and regrow the map on every cell. `ClearPreview` reads the layers again when a preview grew the bounds, shrinking them and the chunks back to the filled cells. A rebuild or disabling the renderer drops the preview along with the control map. The editor previews only cells at z = 0, the only ones the renderer reads.
- **When the preview clears.** The preview is cleared:
  - on commit (`RegisterUndo`, which the Tile Palette calls before a stroke's write: on mouse down for paint and erase, on release for a box or a flood);
  - when the stroke ends, as `executing` turns false on release;
  - on pointer leave, tool deactivation, and undo or redo (the grid brush editor's own handling);
  - when the brush or the paint target changes (`GridPaintingState` events);
  - when a scene closes, before an assembly reload, and when the editor is disabled.

  A new preview replaces the last one by itself. Unity's grid brush editor refreshes a hover by clearing the preview and then previewing again. So during the Scene view's GUI the editor holds a clear back until the end of `OnPaintSceneGUI`, and drops it when a new preview replaced the old one first. A hover then uploads once, and never shrinks a grown map just to grow it again. A clear that does happen asks the Scene view to repaint, or the cleared cells would stay on screen until the next repaint. So does a preview that added chunks, since chunks made in an editor update only draw from the next one.
- **Shift previews an erase.** With shift held, a Paint tool click and the box tool's release erase, so their previews show the cells empty. Unity's own previews show a fill there. Unity's grid brush editor refreshes its preview only when the hovered cells, the target, the tool or the brush change, not the modifier keys. So the editor redoes a hovered paint or box preview itself when shift is pressed or released (`FollowShift`).
- **A hover update allocates nothing** (measured). The brush tests assert that the paint, erase and box previews, and the renderer's preview and clear, allocate nothing once warmed up. A real Scene view GUI pass was measured too: a `SceneView.duringSceneGui` handler drove `OnPaintSceneGUI` with layout events over a temporary grid. 20 hovered cells in a row allocated 0 bytes for the whole path, the grid brush editor's refresh and the Dual Grid Brush editor's module lookup included, and 20 repaint events drawing the outline allocated 0 bytes. Scheduling the upload subscribes to `RenderPipelineManager.beginContextRendering`. That allocates nothing while nothing else listens (nothing did with the Village Forest open), and would allocate a delegate list per update otherwise. In a full test run the first preview cycles allocated once (runtime warm-up), so the tests warm up before measuring. After the review below the same pass gave 0 bytes over 20 hovers again. It also showed that hovering 60 cells past the edge kept the grown bounds from one cell to the next, that leaving the Scene view shrank them back, and that releasing the mouse after a held stroke cleared the preview.
- **Test hooks.** Outside IMGUI `Event.current` is null and the grid brush editor's `OnPaintSceneGUI` cannot run. The tests therefore reach the stroke end through the internal `FollowStroke`, shift through `FollowShift`, and the shifted paint and box through `PaintPreview(…, erasing)` and `BoxPreview`. The editor assembly now grants its internals to the EditMode tests (`Assets/Editor/AssemblyInfo.cs`).
- **A review after the phase** led to these fixes:
  - the editor falls back to the Dual Grid Brush's preview when the renderer is off or has not built;
  - `SetPreview` finds layers among the built tilemaps, not the inspector's list;
  - a shifted Paint tool previews an erase, and a change of shift redoes the preview;
  - a preview that grew the map shrinks back when cleared, while a hover refresh no longer clears in between;
  - the tile writer only serves the material folder, refuses Tile subclasses and albedos smaller than a cell, re-points the tile when refreshing its swatch, and skips unchanged swatches;
  - the renderer's bookkeeping after a control map change (chunks, upload) now lives in one helper.

  The review also pointed out two duplications, which were kept. The preview works out which cells a stroke writes on its own rather than asking the brush, and the `Previews_CoverTheCellsTheClickWrites` test fails if the two disagree. The footprint rule copies `DualGridBrushEditor`'s private `GetBrushBounds`, since sharing it would change DualGrid code.

- EditMode, `TerrainBlendRenderer`: painting a data tilemap updates its texel; an `AreaTerrain` edit, and one applied with `ApplyReplicated`, updates it; disabling unsubscribes and destroys the runtime texture, meshes and material; chunk meshes cover the layers' bounds; nothing allocates per frame when no cell changed; the style and materials are never modified; editing the style or a material rebuilds the renderers using it; a preview shows only in the shown cells, replaces the last, needs the renderer drawing the tilemap and follows the built layers until a rebuild, clears back to the real ones, grows the map and the chunks with one upload and shrinks them back once cleared, and allocates nothing per frame or per move.
- EditMode, `TerrainBrush`: paint, erase, box fill and box erase on plain and dual-grid ground layers write exactly the stroke's cells (the palette's tile or the module's data tile) and undo with the control map; elsewhere it is the Dual Grid Brush, and so is its preview on the layers of a renderer that is off; the editor's previews write preview bits only on the cells the click writes, replace each other, show an erase while shift is held and follow shift changes, clear on stroke end, commit and pointer leave, and allocate nothing; a flood fill shows none; a stroke reaches a built navigation grid in edit mode.
- EditMode, ground tiles: a swatch is its albedo's bottom-left cell; each material has a plain tile without a collider showing its swatch; the ground palettes hold them.
- EditMode, recipes: the same seed gives the same pixels and PNG bytes; the cell field keeps the shader's contract; the height is smooth with one value per chip or stone; the base colour written is the textures' base.
- EditMode, shaders: the terrain shader and its graph compile without errors (`ShaderUtil.ShaderHasError`), with both keywords.
- EditMode, writer: a generated layout written into a scene sets up plain layers and the renderer; navigation still blocks exactly the footprints.
- Art: `TerrainBlendProbe.CheckBlobs` finds no detached blobs or holes on the converted areas (until Phase 4, `compose.py` checks the old path tilesets on its test maps).
- Manual: screenshots of both areas in both looks; seams across cell borders; lighting under the Global Light 2D; Multiplayer Play Mode as in Phase 4.
